Two kinds of freedom: A Corpus-Based Analysis of Meaning in English Discourse

Tava Reese & Aidan Kelley · Department of Linguistics, CU Boulder

LING 5200 Computational Corpus Linguistics · Dec 2025   paper ↗ code ↗ data ↗

In 1958 the philosopher Isaiah Berlin argued that freedom is really two ideas wearing one word: freedom from interference, and freedom to be the master of your own life. We went looking for those two ideas in ten thousand sentences of real political speech, and asked a language model whether it could tell them apart.

TL;DR

Using the ParlaMint-GB corpus of UK parliamentary debates (2015–2020), we pulled 10,000 uses of freedom, counted what follows it, and mapped each occurrence through BERT. The model separates freedom to and freedom from into distinct regions of meaning, and in a logistic regression on speaker metadata, party was the only significant predictor: left-leaning MPs leaned toward freedom from.

01Two concepts of liberty

Berlin's negative liberty is the classical liberal idea: a protected sphere that no person or state may enter. Freedom of speech and freedom of religion live here. His positive liberty asks a different question — am I my own master? — and is about capacity and self-direction rather than the absence of obstacles.

Berlin thought the difference mattered, because positive liberty can be captured: an authority can coerce people in the name of the "true" freedom they would want if they were rational. So we asked whether the distinction actually shows up in how people talk.

We operationalized the two concepts with the two most telling prepositions: freedom from (negative) and freedom to (positive).

02The pipeline

The study ran in three layers, from simple counting to a neural language model to a statistical test. Click through each stage.

03What comes after freedom?

The first pass was plain corpus linguistics. We tokenized the sentences with NLTK and counted the word that directly follows freedom. Three prepositions dominate: of, to, and from, which map almost exactly onto Berlin's distinction.

We set of aside. It's the most common partner by far, but mostly inside fixed names for things — freedom of information, freedom of movement, freedom of speech — where the preposition no longer carries the speaker's framing.

04Asking a language model

Counting prepositions tells you about form. To see whether the meanings separate, we passed every sentence through BERT Base Uncased, a 12-layer encoder, and kept only the vector for the token freedom. Each transformer layer mixes in more information from the surrounding sentence, so the vector that comes out of layer 6 is freedom as colored by its context.

The key design choice was which layer to read from. Or, as we put it in the paper: how long do the embeddings stay in the oven?

Each freedom vector has 768 dimensions. We grouped them with HDBSCAN (minimum cluster size 10) and flattened them to two dimensions with t-SNE (perplexity 30) so a person can look at them. Points that sit close together were close in BERT's representation space; the axes themselves mean nothing.

05The map of freedom

Here is the result: every occurrence of freedom from the 3,000-sentence BERT sample, placed by meaning. Hover a point to read its sentence, click to pin it, or search for a word. Use the buttons to switch between the clusters, the two constructions, and speaker party.

Two things stand out. The largest cluster in the whole map holds the large majority of freedom to sentences, and it also pulls in sentences without the bigram that share its sense of ability: freedom for civil society to operate, freedom for local planning. The model found the concept, not just the preposition.

Meanwhile, freedom from forms a small, tight island of its own. Its most central sentence speaks of a judicial system designed to guarantee neutrality and freedom from political interference, which reads almost like Berlin's own definition of negative liberty. Switch to Party and you'll see the other half of the story: no region of meaning belongs to one party. The difference is in proportion, not territory.

06Who says which?

Finally we fit a binomial logistic regression in R, predicting construction type from speaker metadata:

ConstructionType ~ Gender + Party + Year

Gender and year were not significant. Party was. Left-leaning speakers were 62% less likely to use freedom to than centre or right speakers, favoring freedom from instead.

Over 2015–2019 there's a hint of freedom from gaining ground, but the trend wasn't significant and five years is a short window. It's the first thing we'd test with more data.

07Limits and what's next

Two bigrams are a narrow window. Berlin's concepts can be expressed many ways; freedom of and constructions without a preposition were excluded to keep the model binary.

The map is a sample. Memory limits meant BERT ran on the first 3,000 of 10,000 sentences. Batched inference would fix this.

t-SNE is a flattening. A 2D picture can't preserve every relationship among 768-dimensional vectors, so we read it alongside the counts and the regression, never on its own.

Next: extend to freedom of and other constructions, use a longer time span to test the temporal shift, and run the same pipeline on American congressional speech.
